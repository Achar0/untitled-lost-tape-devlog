# 001 — On Using AI in This Project

This is the first entry in this devlog, so a quick word on what this project is before I get into the thing I actually want to talk about: **untitled** is a 2D pixel-art narrative adventure about a present-day protagonist chasing a "lostwave" song — a piece of music known to have existed but never fully recovered — first mentioned in a 1990s internet forum thread. Underneath that hunt is a quieter story about depression, and about what it looks like to slowly regain the capacity to engage with the world again. More on the design in later posts. For now: AI.

## I used AI to help develop this project

I brainstormed the concept, the mechanics, and a lot of the design language for this game in conversation with an AI assistant. Not as a ghostwriter, and not to generate the game itself — as a sounding board and a way to pressure-test ideas faster than I could alone. I want to say that plainly, up front, rather than let it come out later or bury it in a footnote.

I know this isn't something the industry looks kindly on right now, and I understand why. A lot of AI use in games has meant replacing labor rather than supporting it — generating art, writing, or code wholesale and shipping it with minimal human judgment involved. That's a real concern and I don't think it's an overreaction.

So here's specifically what happened, and what didn't:

- **What AI helped with**: early brainstorming (genre, tone, setting), working through narrative directions, stress-testing whether a mechanic actually said what I wanted it to say, and organizing scattered ideas into something coherent enough to build from.
- **What AI did not do**: write the game's code, art, music, or final narrative text. Those are mine to make, and that's exactly where I intend to keep it.

For what it's worth, Steam's current AI disclosure policy (updated January 2026) draws roughly this same line — it requires disclosure for generative AI content that ships in the game and reaches players, but not for AI-assisted ideation or development tooling that never makes it into the final product. By that standard I technically wouldn't need to disclose this at all. I'm doing it anyway, because I'd rather be upfront about my process than have "technically compliant" be the bar I'm aiming for.

There's also a more personal reason to say this out loud. The game itself is partly *about* this exact tension — a tool that can hand you a complete, comforting, possibly-fabricated version of something, versus the harder work of finding the real, incomplete thing yourself. I'd feel a little ridiculous building that theme into the game and then staying quiet about where AI actually sat in my own process. So: it sat in the brainstorming room, not at the keyboard for the parts that ship. I'll keep being specific about that distinction as this devlog continues.

More soon — next post will get into the actual mechanics.
